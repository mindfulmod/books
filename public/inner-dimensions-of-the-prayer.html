02 · The opening argument

Two tastes, one heart

The book's full title names a comparison. Ibn al-Qayyim's opening paragraph states the relationship as inverse: the stronger the yearning for song, the weaker the attachment to prayer must become.

In one line

His claim is not that song is the lesser pleasure. It is that the two tastes are mutually exclusive — whichever grows strong drives the other out of the heart.

What the author compares

The delight of prayer

Attention to Allah, His words, and the acts of worship.

↔
The competing attachment

The listening he criticises

Musical and poetic gatherings pursued for spiritual rapture.

He argues that strengthening one attachment weakens the other. This is his account of spiritual taste, not a measured relationship or a claim about every use of sound. His positive account of listening to the Qur’an appears in the comparison section.

Why the order of the book matters

Chapters 1 and 2 spend roughly three-quarters of the book building up the interior of the prayer in minute detail. Only then do Chapters 3–5 turn and measure the rival pleasure against it. The sustained account of prayer supplies the standard for the comparison that follows.

A point to keep in mind

That this is a book about music. It is a book about the prayer — most of its length develops the inward meanings of prayer. The comparison is the frame, not the subject.

For reflection
  • Which of the two tastes have you actually cultivated deliberately?
  • Which habits strengthen your attention during prayer?
  • Could you describe the taste of your own prayer to someone who asked?

03 · Chapter One §1.1

The invitation to prayer

Ibn al-Qayyim opens by refusing the frame of prayer as a tax on the day. It is a gift Allah sent His Messenger ﷺ to deliver, given “not out of any need of them, but as a favour from Him and a bounty upon them.”

In one line

The prayer is presented as a generous invitation to a banquet. The invitation comes five times a day. Within each prayer, its different acts nourish the heart in different ways.

Six names, in one sentence

The treatise opens with these, before any argument. Note that the last two are not comforts at all — they are instruments for finding out what something is worth.

muḥibbīn

The coolness of their eyes

Of those who love Him — the Arabic idiom for what a person's eye rests on with relief.

muwaḥḥidīn

The delight of their spirits

Of those who affirm His oneness.

ʿābidīn

Their garden

Of the worshippers — the word is bustān, a walled and watered orchard.

khāshiʿīn

The delight of their souls

Of those who are humbled before Him.

ṣādiqīn

The touchstone

Of the states of the truthful. Miḥakk is the assayer's stone — you rub the metal on it to see what it really is.

sālikīn

The balance

On which the states of those travelling the road are weighed.

The banquet

Because people were created with appetites and exposed to temptation, His mercy required that “He laid out for him a banquet gathering every kind of dish, every rarity, every robe of honour and every gift” — and called him to it five times every day. Each dish, meaning each action of the prayer, carries “a pleasure, a benefit and a dignity that is not in the others.”

Before the meal their hearts endured “hunger, drought, thirst, nakedness and sickness”; they leave “fed and watered, clothed in the robes of acceptance, and made rich.”

What the prayer does

  • Atones for minor sins
  • Brings light and strength into the heart
  • Increases one's share of provision in this life
  • Inspires others to love the one who prays
  • Makes the angels — and the earth with its mountains, trees and rivers — rejoice
  • On the Day of Judgement, turns into light and transforms into reward
A point to keep in mind

That calling the prayer a “gift” is a pious flourish. The image explains both variety and renewal: each act offers a different benefit, and the invitation returns as the need returns.

For reflection
  • Do you arrive at the prayer as an invited guest or as a debtor?
  • Can you name what one particular action of the prayer gives you that no other does?
  • What did your heart carry into the prayer that it should not carry out?

04 · Chapter One §1.2–1.3

The heart is a piece of land

The heart is compared to land, and mercy to rain. Heedlessness dries the soil; renewed remembrance restores its growth. The branches represent the limbs: their readiness for worship depends on the life of the heart.

In one line

The heart is a field. Remembrance is rain, heedlessness is drought — and the damage is proportionate, not absolute.

A closer look

What changes when the rain returns?

Follow the change from dryness to rain and new growth, and what each means for the heart and limbs.

Heedlessness dries the soil

The source compares neglect of remembrance to drought. Its effect varies with the degree of heedlessness; the image is a warning about a condition, not a permanent verdict on a person.

Remembrance neglected→Heart loses vitality→Limbs resist good action

Mercy renews the heart

Turning back to Allah and remembering Him are compared to rain reaching dry land. The passage explicitly returns from drought to growth: renewal remains central to the image.

Turning back→Rain reaches the soil→Growth resumes

The branches respond

The limbs are the branches. When the heart is watered, they become responsive to worship and each bears its own fruit. The point is the connection between inward life and outward action.

A nourished heart→Responsive limbs→Fruits of worship

Editorial study aid · Read the Arabic passage

Why does the author compare the limbs to branches?

The condition of the heart affects their readiness for worship, just as water reaching a tree affects its branches.

Why the invitation repeats — and why it pauses

Hearts endure recurring sterility, so the call is renewed five times daily. But mercy also required a gap between each call — so the one invited keeps asking for rain “the One in whose hand is the rain of hearts,” rather than assuming it.

“The drought that falls on the heart is heedlessness. Heedlessness is the drought of hearts and their barrenness.” The moment a servant becomes heedless, drought strikes — and the damage is proportionate to the degree of heedlessness.

When the land dies

A parched heart yields nothing; “the burning of appetites works through it from every side like a scorching wind.” The limbs — the branches — grow rigid and brittle, useless even as branches.

At which point “the judgement of the garden's keeper calls for that tree to be cut down and made into firewood.”

Five absences that dry the heart

  1. The oneness of Allah — tawḥīd
  2. Love of Him
  3. Knowledge of Him
  4. Remembrance of Him — dhikr
  5. Calling upon Him — duʿāʾ
A point to keep in mind

That drought is a permanent verdict on a person. It is explicitly proportionate — “in proportion to his heedlessness, little or much” — and the whole image turns on the rain returning.

For reflection
  • What helps you return to remembrance after distraction?
  • Which of the five — oneness, love, knowledge, remembrance, calling on Him — has actually gone quiet?
  • Which good action becomes easier when you regularly remember Allah?

05 · Chapter One §1.4–1.6

Three ways of using what we are given

Sorted by what they do with their limbs. The order is counter-intuitive: the idle are placed below the actively disobedient.

In one line

The three examples distinguish caring for a trust, using it destructively, and wasting it. The author’s severe ranking draws attention to the loss involved in neglecting both worldly and religious responsibility.

These are the author’s moral examples of how entrusted resources are used. They concern chosen neglect and misuse, not inability, illness, disability, or needed rest.

One · the mindful

Limbs in the service of their Lord

Those who have “traded with Allah in the most profitable trade” who made obedience their trade. In prayer the whole body follows the lead of the heart, each limb practising its own act of worship.

Parable: a man given land, tools, seed and water. He plants, fences it, hires guards, keeps watch, replaces dead plants, pulls weeds, reinvests the profit.

Two · the treacherous

Limbs turned to disobedience

They use their limbs for what they were not created for. Their trade fails; they earn punishment and are cut off from reward.

Parable: a man who made the same land a den for wild beasts, a dump for corpses, a hideout for thieves — and handed his tools to those living there.

Three · the heedless

Limbs suspended entirely

“A greater loss than the one before him.” They serve neither the purpose of their creation nor even worldly good. “The most hateful of creatures to Allah is the idle servant — neither at work for this world nor striving for the next.”

Parable: a man who neglected the land, wasted the water in the desert, and was left regretful.

The reasoning behind the ranking

“If a man who strives for this world and not the next is already blameworthy and abandoned, what then of one who has shut down both?” His criticism concerns neglected responsibility and wasted opportunity.

The man at the king's door · §1.6

A man goes to the King to apologise and to beg rain for his heart. At the very doorstep he turns away and busies himself with what the King most dislikes — yet still sends his servants, the limbs, to stand in “to make his excuses and deputise for him.”

Mercy still grants him something. But “there is a difference between dividing the spoils among those who hold a rightful share, and the token handed to one who has no share at all.”

A point to keep in mind

The third example concerns deliberately wasting entrusted resources. Read the severe comparison in that context; it is not a judgement about people who are unable to work or who need rest.

For reflection
  • Which of the three describes how you used your body this week?
  • What would caring for your responsibilities look like today?
  • Are your limbs guarded, spent, or simply idle?

Editorial study aid · Read the Arabic passage

Why pause after bowing instead of rushing to prostration?

The standing after bowing is an intended pillar in its own right, with praise and gratitude as its particular focus.

The unit has the shape of the first revelation

The first chapter revealed, al-ʿAlaq, opens with “Recite in the name of your Lord who created” and closes with “But prostrate and draw near.”

“It opened with recitation and closed with prostration — and the rakʿah was laid out on that pattern: its beginning recitation, its end prostration.”

Five pillars — each one names the whole prayer

Ibn al-Qayyim's proof that no pillar is decorative: the Qur'an calls the prayer itself by the name of each pillar in turn.

  • Recitation · 17:78, 73:20  ·  Standing · 73:2, 2:238
  • Bowing · 2:43, 77:48  ·  Prostration · 15:98, 96:19
  • Remembrance · 62:9, 63:9

Most honourable action: prostration. Most honourable remembrance: recitation of the Qur'an.

A point to keep in mind

That prostration is simply the humblest posture, and therefore the low point of the prayer. In his account it is the summit — the position of nearest approach — which is why he gives particular attention to its repetition within each rakʿah.

For reflection
  • In your last prostration, were you aware it was the closest point?
  • Does your bowing feel like a transition or like a pillar?
  • Which station do you habitually rush?

Wuḍūʾ

Purification
Outward

Dirt is cleansed from the body, and from the very limbs the prayer will use.

Inward

Purification of the heart from sin through repentance. The two are joined in 2:222, which pairs those who repent with those who purify.

Coming to the masjid

Entering His house
Outward

Attending the congregation — which some scholars hold obligatory, others strongly recommended.

Inward

Purification prepares the worshipper to return to Allah. In the author’s image, the heedless servant comes back to the One he had neglected and stands before Him in humility.

The opening takbīr

Takbīrat al-Iḥrām
Outward

Facing the qiblah and declaring Allah’s greatness with an attitude of surrender. Ibn al-Qayyim’s description here concerns humility before Allah.

Inward

The meaning is that Allah is greater than whatever is in the heart — and it is made true by dismissing that thing. If something still occupies you, Ibn al-Qayyim says, “that shows that thing is greater in his estimation than Allah,” and the words were “on his tongue and not in his heart.”

It also “takes him out of wearing the cloak of pride, which contradicts servanthood.”

Opening supplication, then refuge

Istiftāḥ · Istiʿādhah
Outward

Subḥānaka Allāhumma wa biḥamdika… — glorification, repeated. Then seeking refuge from Satan before reciting.

Inward

Praise turns the heart back from heedlessness. Satan’s aim is first to prevent the prayer, and failing that, to ensure the heart gains nothing from it — which is why refuge is sought precisely at the point of recitation.

His gloss on why refuge is commanded: it is as though it were said to him — you have no power against this enemy; take refuge in Me and I will shelter you from him, and I will suffice you against him and shield you from him. He adds a warning — to address Allah with the heart elsewhere is to be like a man granted audience with a king who then turns his back while speaking.

al-Fātiḥah

The response in the hadith
Outward

Reciting the opening chapter, verse by verse.

Inward

The practical instruction: pause briefly at the end of each verse and attend to the response reported in the hadith. Prayer is structured as dialogue — he cites the ḥadīth qudsī in which Allah divides the chapter between Himself and His servant.

A closer look

Recite, pause, understand

Choose a passage. The reveal shows the response transmitted in the hadith; it does not suggest waiting to hear an audible reply.

الحمد لله رب العالمين

All praise belongs to Allah, Lord of the worlds.

Reveal the response reported in the hadith

“My servant has praised Me.”

What to attend to

Praise acknowledges the perfection of Allah’s names, attributes, and actions. “Lord of the worlds” joins His creation and care of all things to His unique right to be worshipped.

الرحمن الرحيم

The Most Compassionate, the Most Merciful.

Reveal the response reported in the hadith

“My servant has extolled Me.”

What to attend to

The author moves from mercy embracing creation to the particular mercy of being enabled to stand in prayer. Repeated praise of His attributes is thanāʾ: extolling Him.

مالك يوم الدين

Master of the Day of Recompense.

Reveal the response reported in the hadith

“My servant has glorified Me.”

What to attend to

Remembering judgement calls for humility, justice, and restraint from wrongdoing. Tamjīd is praise through attributes of greatness and majesty.

إياك نعبد وإياك نستعين

You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help.

Reveal the response reported in the hadith

“This is between Me and My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks.”

What to attend to

Worship is the end sought; Allah’s help is the means. The verse moves from praise to direct address, joining sincere intention with dependence on Him.

Reveal the response reported in the hadith

“This is for My servant, and My servant shall have what he asks.”

What to attend to

The concluding request includes the whole passage to the end of the sūrah. Guidance includes knowing, intending, acting, remaining steady, and calling others with patience.

Why nothing can be substituted

Each act and word of the prayer carries its own distinct effect on the heart, which is why no substitution is possible: the takbīr and al-Fātiḥah cannot be exchanged for other words, any more than standing, bowing and prostrating could be exchanged for other movements.

On al-ḥamd

“No leaf falls except by His praise, and no atom moves in the universe except by His praise.” And because praise is itself a favour, praising Him obliges praising Him again. Al-Awzāʿī heard: “To You belongs praise — whether for a blessing given, or for an affliction turned away.”

Worship is the aim; help makes it possible

In “You alone we worship,” the worshipper directs knowledge, love, intention, and action to Allah. “You alone we ask for help” acknowledges dependence on Him in every affair. The author places worship first because it is Allah’s right and the purpose sought; assistance is what the servant needs to fulfil it.

The repeated “You alone” makes both clauses exclusive. Their position matters too: they stand between praise and petition. Ibn al-Qayyim describes this pairing of purpose and means as a centre around which the Qur’an’s teaching turns.

Ten kinds of guidance the servant needs

Note how few concern a disbeliever becoming a believer. Almost all describe a guided person still needing guidance — which is why the request is set in the five daily prayers, “in the best of his states.”

  1. Things done outside guidance in knowledge, act or intent — he needs to repent of them, and the repentance is itself guidance
  2. Things he was guided to in principle but not in detail
  3. Things he was guided to in one respect and not another — he needs completion, and to be “increased in guidance upon his guidance”
  4. Guidance in what is still to come, as he was given it in what has passed
  5. Matters in which he holds no belief at all
  6. Matters in which he believes the opposite of the truth — he needs a guidance that erases the false belief and sets its contrary in its place
  7. Things he is able to do but has no will for — he needs a will created in him
  8. Things he wills but is unable to do — he needs to be given the power
  9. Things he neither wills nor is able to do — he needs both created
  10. Things he already holds rightly — he needs to be kept steady in them and to continue

Āmīn, then the movement into bowing

Outward

Conclude the request with āmīn. The author then discusses raising the hands and the takbīr of transition.

Inward

Āmīn affirms the petition and expresses hope for its acceptance. The raised hands share in worship; the recurring takbīr renews the declaration of Allah’s greatness as the prayer moves from act to act.

Bowing

al-Rukūʿ
Outward

Back bent, head lowered, tongue saying Subḥāna Rabbiya'l-ʿAẓīm — “Glory to my Lord, the Magnificent.” The hands are raised beforehand: deference to the command, servitude of the hands themselves, following the Prophet ﷺ, and part of what beautifies the prayer.

Inward

He “makes himself small and slight before his Lord” and empties the heart of any glorification of anyone besides Allah. When the heart fills with His glorification, glorification of self is crowded out.

The author brings the three together: bowing gathers “the submission of the heart, the submission of the limbs and the submission of speech, in their fullest state.” And: “submission is the attribute of the servant, and greatness is the attribute of the Lord.”

Rising from bowing

al-Qiyām
Outward

Standing upright again, praising and exalting.

Inward

Attention turns “to the praise of his Lord, to extolling and glorifying Him, and to His being alone in giving and withholding.”

A full pillar, not a transition — which is why the Prophet ﷺ prolonged it as he prolonged bowing and prostration, repeating “To my Lord be all praise” in the night prayer until the standing was as long as the bowing.

Prostration

al-Sujūd — the closest point
Outward

The forehead set on the ground; the face — “the noblest thing he has” — brought down into the dust, the nose humbled with it. No limb rests on another: thighs off the shins, belly off the thighs, upper arms off the sides.

Inward

Every limb is separated so each takes its own share of submission rather than being carried by another. When the heart's state matches the body's, “the heart prostrated to the Lord as the body prostrated — and with it prostrated his nose, his face, his hands, his knees and his feet.”

The five requests

The heart and the nafs are partners

“The heart is a partner of the nafs in good and evil, in reward and punishment, in praise and blame.” The nafs by nature “runs from the yoke of servanthood.” What happens is reciprocal and settled by strength: if the nafs is the stronger, the heart becomes its partner and its captive — and if the heart is the stronger, the nafs becomes its partner and its captive.

The second prostration

Why twice, when bowing is once
The reason

Because of its honour, and because it is the point of nearest approach. Prostration is “more renowned in servanthood and more deeply rooted in it” than the other pillars, so it is made the seal of the rakʿah and what precedes it is preamble. Bowing is placed before it, he says, as “a gradual passage from a thing to what is higher than it.”

Why anything repeats

The acts are “the food of the heart and the spirit, which have no standing without them.” One mouthful does not satisfy hunger — “it might well open the door of hunger wider than before.” The second is “put in the place of thanks for the first,” and the repetition scours the heart “like washing a garment time after time.”

He compares prostration's place in the prayer to the ṭawāf of visitation in the pilgrimage — as the servant is nearest his Lord in prostration, so the pilgrim is nearest in circling. Ibn ʿUmar, asked for his daughter's hand while circling, gave no answer; when he had finished he said: “Do you mention a matter of this world, while we are presenting ourselves before Allah in our ṭawāf?”

Tashahhud, salutations, closing

al-Taḥiyyāt · al-Taslīm
Outward

Sitting to greet; then peace upon the Prophet ﷺ, upon oneself, and upon the righteous; the two testimonies; prayers upon the Prophet ﷺ; then free supplication; then the salām.

Inward

The definite article on al-Taḥiyyāt gathers every kind of greeting ever offered to a king and assigns the whole class to Allah alone. The word derives from ḥayāh, life — affirming the perpetuity of the One greeted.

Protocol

Greeting the created never precedes greeting the Creator — matching 27:59. Within it, rank orders the recipients: the Messenger ﷺ first, “whose nation received every form of good only through him,” then the worshipper himself, then the rest.

The order of asking

Praise, then prayers upon the Prophet ﷺ, then request — per Fuḍālah ibn ʿUbayd. Only then “the servant has the choice and freedom to ask Allah for what he wants.” The chapter ends with a related five-part response to the adhān, set out below.

What the closing words gather

Al-Taḥiyyāt

All honours belong to Allah, the living and enduring King. The author gathers the different forms of royal greeting into an acknowledgement of His sovereignty.

Al-Ṣalawāt

All prayers belong to Him. Honours acknowledge His kingship; prayer expresses the worship owed to Him.

Al-Ṭayyibāt

Good words and deeds belong to Him. The term also points to His goodness: what is attributed to Him is good, and He accepts what is good.

The words of remembrance

Subḥān Allāh declares Him free of imperfection; al-ḥamdu lillāh praises His perfection; lā ilāha illā Allāh affirms worship of Him alone; Allāhu akbar declares His greatness above all things; lā ḥawla wa lā quwwata illā billāh acknowledges that power and strength come through Him. The author compares seeking ultimate refuge elsewhere to sheltering in a spider’s fragile house.

The two testimonies

The testimony to Allah’s oneness belongs with the testimony to the Messenger ﷺ. They affirm the foundation on which the prayer rests, just as the testimonies conclude purification and are sought at the end of life. The treatise records different legal readings of when the prayer is “completed”; its point here is the place of testimony near the conclusion.

08 · Chapter Two

Two qiblahs

He is “commanded to face the Sacred House with his face, and to face Allah with his heart.” Just as the face may not turn from the qiblah, the heart may not turn to anything but Him.

In one line

Three forms of attention belong together: guarding the heart, remembering before Whom you stand, and understanding the words and acts of prayer.

A closer look

Three forms of attention, one prayer

Select a focus. The three belong together; none makes the others unnecessary.

GuardAttendUnderstandThe whole heart turns toward Allah

Guard and repair the heart

Attend to desires, whisperings, and passing thoughts that diminish or undo the prayer’s reward. This work remains necessary while you attend to Allah and to the meanings of the prayer.

Remember before Whom you stand

Murāqaba means watchfulness: worshipping with awareness of Allah, as though seeing Him. This is attention to the One worshipped, not the production of a mental image.

Understand the words and acts

Attend to the meanings of Allah’s speech and to what each act of prayer asks of the heart. Understanding helps give the prayer its due humility and stillness.

Editorial study aid · Read the Arabic passage

Does understanding the words replace guarding the heart?

No. The passage gathers all three forms of attention into the establishment of prayer.

Submission in two kinds of circumstance

The author distinguishes Allah’s decree in what befalls a person from His command concerning what that person must do. Both call for a response of servanthood: meeting circumstances faithfully and obeying the command.

Submission to decree does not mean following desire and then excusing wrongdoing by saying it was destined. This distinction in Chapter Two explains taslīm as surrender, beyond the closing salutation.

Why the word “in” matters

The Prophet ﷺ never said the delight of his eye lay in fasting, ḥajj or ʿumrah — he said “the delight of my eye has been placed in the prayer.” Ibn al-Qayyim presses the preposition: he did not say by the prayer. The delight is not had except by entering into it, “as the eye of the lover is delighted by being joined to the one he loves” — never from outside it. Hence: “Bilāl, give us rest by the prayer.”

He notes a second omission: the Prophet ﷺ did not say “give us rest from it” — which is exactly the posture of those who pray to discharge an obligation.

Two people performing the same prayer

Comfort found in the prayer

  • Finds it “a delight to his heart” — tranquility, calmness, pleasure
  • The prayer is “a garden”
  • Receives not only reward but “a particular nearness and closeness, and a high standing with Allah”
  • At the final sitting, “would that it joined up with the Day of Meeting”

Parable: a man admitted to the king with the screen removed — delighting in looking at him, brought near, unable to bear leaving.

Comfort found outside the prayer

  • Prays “in a rush,” compelled only by knowing it is obligatory
  • The tongue says what the heart does not — he says “with the tongue of his heart: let us pray so that we may be rested from the prayer, not by it”
  • Experiences it as “a heavy shackle on his limbs, and a narrow, cramped prison for his heart”
  • May still have sins forgiven — “and he may be punished for what he left deficient in it”

Parable: a man who enters the king's abode but cannot reach him, a screen between them — the screen being his own desires.

A point to keep in mind

That the three levels are a ladder you climb once and leave behind. They are three objects of attention, and the first — turning to your own heart to guard and repair it — never stops being required.

For reflection
  • Which kind of attention could you give more care to in your next prayer?
  • Does your comfort arrive when the prayer begins, or when it ends?
  • At the final sitting, are you reluctant to leave?

09 · Chapters Three to Five

Prayer weighed against listening

In Chapter Five, Ibn al-Qayyim asks the reader to consider what remains after the listening ends. His account moves from absorbed pleasure to its after-effect, then to the means through which that pleasure was received.

In one line

His objection concerns both the means of receiving pleasure and its consequences. Even a real benefit, he argues, can arrive mixed with harm.

During the gathering

Absorbed in pleasure

In the author’s account, enjoyment can prevent the listener from noticing a harmful mixture.

→
After it ends

The after-effect is noticed

He describes contraction, estrangement, and the need to turn back and seek a remedy.

This sequence represents his argument about the listening under discussion. It is not a mood test or a measurement of anyone’s faith.

What happens

Why the effect is noticed later

If a man's heart is wholly taken up “with the sight of a beloved, or the sight of something feared, or a pleasure that has seized his senses and his heart,” and in that state he is struck or stung, “he scarcely feels it.” When the state lifts he finds the pain “as though it had struck him at that very moment.”

The mixture

Clear and muddy water

At its highest, he says, such listening is “mixed of truth and falsehood, compounded of appetite and doubt.” So in the valley of the heart “the small clear water mixes with the large turbid water, and the foul overcomes the good” — what comes from the Most Merciful running alongside what comes from Satan.

The response

Renewing repentance

Some of the truthful “hurried, on leaving it, to renew repentance and seek forgiveness, and to take up the remedies by which the causes of that contraction and estrangement are driven off.”

A closer look

Look at the means as well as the feeling

Choose a comparison. A pleasant experience alone does not answer how it was obtained or what it leaves behind.

Pure drink, clean vessel

The sound heart receives good through a fitting means. The author’s positive example is attentive listening to the Qur’an.

Foul drink, dirty vessel

The diseased heart receives what harms it through a corrupt means. Enjoyment by itself does not establish the value of what is received.

A mixture must be distinguished

A heart may contain both faith and hypocrisy; the source also distinguishes mixed vessels and mixed drinks. Earlier, honey in a dirty cup illustrates a real good received through an unsuitable means. These related images should not be collapsed into one.

Editorial study aid · Read the Arabic passage

The listening he does approve

Chapter Four locates spiritual delight across the practices of the Prophet ﷺ and the early generations: prayer, recitation and listening, learning with scholars, striving in Allah’s cause, enjoining good, and loyalties ordered by faith. Its contrast concerns the practices by which the heart is formed, as well as the sound being heard.

The section سماع أهل الحق — “the listening of the people of truth” — gives the counter-model. When the Companions wanted their hearts stirred they would ask one of their number to recite the Qur'an while the rest listened — their hearts would find the taste of faith.

ʿUmar would say to Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī: “O Abū Mūsā, remind us of our Lord.” ʿUthmān said: “If our hearts were pure, we would never have enough of the words of Allah.”

The author’s conclusion

Whoever grasps the objectives of the law — “the purposes of the law in blocking the means that lead to the forbidden” — “has settled the matter that this listening is forbidden.” Things are prohibited on two grounds: for the corruption in them intrinsically, or because they are a means to it.

Reported here as the book's position. Ibn al-Qayyim's ruling is one view within a longer scholarly discussion the treatise itself does not survey.

A point to keep in mind

That he denies listening any good whatsoever. He explicitly grants the truthful listener “a sound taste of faith.” The objection is to the vessel it arrives in — and to what is left once you put it down.

For reflection
  • What do you reach for when you want your heart moved?
  • What remains after the experience ends, beyond the pleasure of the moment?
  • Would you accept the same drink from a different vessel?

10 · What the prayer repairs

The gap between the prayers

Having set out the taste of the prayer, Ibn al-Qayyim turns to why it has to keep happening. His answer is that the prayer is not only an ascent — it is a repair, and the interval between prayers is deliberate.

In one line

Prayer renews what distraction and wrongdoing have weakened. The interval allows the worshipper to recover before returning again.

What erodes it

He names four things that “require of him the loss of his share, or its diminishing”: the soul that commands to evil, the desire that demands what it wants, the dispositions that press their claims, and the Satan who misleads.

Against these the prayer is prescribed as something that “makes good what was lost to him, gives back what went from him, and renews for him what has worn away of his resolve and his faith.”

Why there is a gap at all

The interval is not an oversight. He calls it a barzakh of time between every two prayers, placed there “out of wisdom and mercy” — so the servant may gather himself again; the next prayer cleanses what has accumulated in between.

The same logic runs through the whole book: the invitation repeats because the need repeats.

The form matches the content

He notes that the prayer's outward shape was “made to be the shape of its own meaning” — humility, submission, compliance and surrender — and that every limb was given “its share of servanthood.”

Its fruit and its spirit, though, remain one thing: “his turning toward his Lord in it, with the whole of himself.”

Dressing for an audience

The passage ends with the image of preparing for an audience. The reward and the place of the prayer is “entering upon Him” — and you are told to adorn yourself before doing so.

Ibn al-Qayyim says why: it is “a reminder of the Greater Presentation before Him on the Day of Resurrection.” Preparing for prayer recalls accountability before Allah.

A point to keep in mind

Reward and renewal belong together here. The passage describes prayer restoring resolve and faith, as well as cleansing wrongdoing. It does not turn the interval into a reason to neglect the heart.

For reflection
  • Can you name what the interval since your last prayer actually cost you?
  • Do you arrive at the prayer with something to hand over, or only with the obligation?
  • If the next prayer is a presentation, would you be content to be seen as you currently are?

11 · Supplementary reading

Humility begins in the heart

The English edition appends two extracts from other works of Ibn al-Qayyim. They develop a related question: how does inward humility become visible, and how can its appearance be imitated?

In one line

Outward stillness can express humility, but appearance alone does not establish the state of the heart.

Appendix I · What khushūʿ means

Khushūʿ includes humility, lowliness, and stillness. The extract begins with the Qur’anic call for hearts to yield to remembrance (57:16), the description of humble prayer (23:1–2), humbled voices (20:108), and bare earth renewed by water (41:39).

Its central distinction is between the heart’s submission and the signs that follow. Accepting a true correction is one fruit of humility; quieter conduct can be another. The collected sayings warn against displaying more humility than one has, and against confusing solemn posture with sincerity.

The example of ʿUmar makes the point concrete: energetic walking, an audible voice, and generous action need not conflict with inward humility. The practical question is whether conduct grows from truthfulness before Allah, rather than a wish to look devout.

Source: Madārij al-Sālikīn, the station of khushūʿ. English appendix pp. 90–94; its citation is 1:417–418 in the edition it uses. The English editor marks the report about playing with a beard as fabricated; it is not used here as Prophetic evidence.

Appendix II · Humility and its imitation

From the heart outward

Reverence, love, awareness of blessings, and recognition of one’s wrongdoing humble the heart. The limbs then express that inward condition.

Inward humility → outward expression

An outward performance

A person can deliberately display stillness while the heart remains preoccupied with pride or desire. The appearance has become a performance.

Outward appearance ≠ proof of humility

The low-lying land image belongs here, in al-Rūḥ: water flows into a low place and settles there. The receptive heart is compared with this ground; pride is compared with raised ground that does not retain the water. This is distinct from the drought-and-renewal image in the main treatise.

Use the distinction for reflection on one’s own intention. It does not make another person’s sincerity visible to an observer.

Source: Al-Rūḥ, distinction between humility of faith and hypocritical humility. English appendix pp. 95–96; its citation is pp. 346–347 in the edition it uses.

Can you infer sincerity from a lowered head?

No. The extracts connect inward humility with outward conduct, but warn that the outward appearance can also be imitated.

Corrections and differences worth knowing
  • Ten guidance cases: Arabic heading 25 includes willingness without ability. English p. 45 lists nine and omits that separate case. All ten are retained here.
  • Five things that nourish the heart: tawḥīd begins the Arabic list. English p. 20 puts it last and adds “most importantly.” This guide follows the Arabic ordering.
  • Thanāʾ and tamjīd: the Arabic distinguishes repeated praise from praise of majesty. The English wording on p. 40 blurs that distinction. The al-Fātiḥah study follows the Arabic and the hadith.
  • Bowing: the Arabic says al-ʿAẓīm, the Magnificent. The English text on p. 68 says “Most High” although its transliteration says al-ʿAẓīm. The distinction is preserved here.
  • Submission to decree: the Arabic warns against excusing sin by fate. The wording at the end of English §2.3 (p. 70) obscures the warning; it is restored in the explanation.
  • Land metaphors: drought and rain belong to the main treatise. The low basin that retains water belongs to the second appendix from al-Rūḥ.
  • The banquet: five daily invitations are distinct from the varied dishes, which stand for the acts within prayer.
Scope, reports, and editorial choices

The illustrated guide is selective. The companion preserves the complete retrieved Arabic prose and notes, and explains all 59 passages in English, including examples and reports omitted from the earlier guide. Repeated English phrasing is sometimes combined; this is not a word-for-word translation. The printed biography (pp. 10–15) is condensed to orientation; its full teacher lists, bibliography, and testimonials are not reproduced.

Two “O son of Adam” reports on English p. 26 are explicitly marked by its editor as untraced in hadith books. They are retained in companion passage 8 and clearly marked as untraced, not authenticated divine speech. The appendix’s beard report is likewise not used as Prophetic evidence. Other sayings remain attributed as sayings rather than upgraded to hadith.

The familiar five levels of prayer from al-Wābil al-Ṣayyib are outside this treatise and these two appendices. They are not added as though they belonged to this book.

Diagrams, scenario examples, reflection questions, and generated illustrations are editorial aids. They describe qualitative relationships and do not measure faith, diagnose a reader, or depict unseen realities. The author’s position on musical listening is reported in its own context.

Passage 1 of 59 · English edition pp. 16

The comparison that frames the treatise

The opening invocation asks the generous Lord to make the undertaking easy and grant help. The text is introduced as the words of Muhammad ibn Abi Bakr, Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah, with a prayer for mercy upon him.

The author sets out to compare the inward experience of prayer and Qur’anic recitation with that of musical listening. He argues that these two experiences differ in every respect: as the taste and influence of one grow stronger, those of the other weaken. This is the argument he will develop, rather than a conclusion the reader is asked to assume without reading it.

Passage 2 of 59 · English edition pp. 17–18

Prayer as a gift and a banquet

Prayer is described as the comfort of those who love Allah, the delight of those who affirm His oneness, the garden of worshippers, the joy of the humble, the touchstone of the truthful, and the balance by which the states of spiritual travellers are weighed. It is mercy given to believing servants.

Allah guides them to it and sends this gift through His truthful, trustworthy Messenger. Its purpose is their honour and nearness to Him. He has no need of them. Both heart and limbs worship, but the knowing heart has the greater share: turning to its Lord, rejoicing in His nearness, delighting in His love, standing gladly before Him, turning away from other attachments, and fulfilling inward and outward servanthood in a manner pleasing to Him.

Because the servant is tested by desires and temptations from within and without, Allah’s mercy prepares a banquet containing many dishes, treasures, robes of honour, and gifts. The invitation comes five times each day. Every dish represents an act of prayer with a distinct pleasure, benefit, purpose, and dignity. These different acts bring different honours; each also answers a blameworthy act and brings its particular light.

Prayer brings light and strength to heart and limbs, breadth of provision, and love among people. The author also describes the angels rejoicing, and the earth’s places, mountains, trees, and rivers becoming sources of light and reward at the servant’s meeting with Allah.

Before the banquet, the heart has known hunger, drought, thirst, nakedness, and illness. It leaves fed, watered, clothed in acceptance, and enriched with gifts. The repeated invitation and the variety within each invitation are separate parts of the metaphor.

Passage 3 of 59 · English edition pp. 19–20

The heart as land

Drought repeatedly returns to hearts and souls. Mercy therefore renews the invitation to prayer. Throughout life, the servant keeps asking the One who sends rain upon hearts for the water of His generosity. The aim is to preserve the plants of faith, excellence, and their fruits, and to keep their nourishment flowing into heart and spirit. The servant acknowledges dryness and continual need.

Heedlessness is this drought. While the servant remembers Allah and turns toward Him, mercy falls like recurring rain. Neglect brings dryness in proportion to its extent. When neglect takes firm hold, the land becomes desolate, the year barren, and desires burn through it like scorching winds.

When mercy returns, the land of faith and action stirs, rises, and produces beautiful growth. The tree depends on water for its moisture, greenness, flexibility, and fruit. Without it, roots dry, branches wither, and fruit is withheld. A branch pulled toward you may no longer bend: it breaks. If the tree itself dies, the gardener’s judgement calls for cutting it down for fuel. This severe ending belongs to the author’s warning; it should not erase the earlier description of renewal.

Passage 4 of 59 · English edition pp. 20–21

What nourishes the heart and the limbs

The heart dries when it lacks affirmation of Allah’s oneness, love of Him, knowledge of Him, remembrance, and supplication. The heat of the self and the fire of desires then prevent the branches—the limbs—from responding. The author connects this to the warning about hearts hardened against remembrance in Qur’an 39:22.

When mercy waters the heart, the branches become supple and responsive. Directed toward Allah’s command, they move willingly and yield the fruit of worship. Their nourishment comes from the heart’s life. When that supply fails, their good actions cease. Each limb has a particular obedience required of it: it was created and prepared for that service.

Passage 5 of 59 · English edition pp. 21–22

Three uses of the limbs

The first person uses the limbs for the purpose for which they were created. This is the most profitable trade with Allah and the best sale of oneself to Him. Prayer brings every limb into worship under the heart’s direction. Such a person recognises the gift of those limbs, worships inwardly and outwardly, and protects them from what displeases the Lord or disgraces the servant before Him.

The second confines the limbs to disobedience and wrongdoing. The author describes this as failed effort and a losing trade: the person loses divine pleasure and abundant reward and incurs anger and punishment.

The third disables the limbs through idleness and ignorance. The author calls this an even greater loss, because people were created for worship and obedience, not purposeless inactivity. He condemns a person who undertakes neither worldly work nor effort for the next life and becomes a burden upon both. If striving only for this life while neglecting the next is already blameworthy, neglecting both is worse. The passage ends with that thought expressed in verse. Its criticism concerns the chosen neglect described here, not an assessment of illness or disability.

Passage 6 of 59 · English edition pp. 23

Three people entrusted with land

The first is given a broad piece of land, tools, seed, and sufficient water. He prepares and plants it, cultivates different crops and fruit trees, builds a protective wall, appoints guards, and strengthens it against damage and intruders. Each day he repairs what has deteriorated, replaces dead planting, removes weeds and thorns, and uses its produce to sustain further cultivation.

The second turns the same land into a shelter for beasts and vermin, a place for corpses and foul waste, and a stronghold for thieves and harmful people. The resources intended for cultivation instead support those who corrupt the land.

The third abandons the land and lets the water run uselessly into the desert, ending in blame and regret. These are the author’s examples of watchfulness, betrayal, and heedlessness.

Passage 7 of 59 · English edition pp. 23–24

The same daily actions, different intentions

The watchful person is prepared for the purpose of creation. Moving or resting, standing or sitting, eating or drinking, sleeping or dressing, speaking or remaining silent—all can count in that person’s favour as remembrance, obedience, nearness, and increase.

For the person who betrays the trust, those activities count against them and deepen estrangement and loss. Allah did not grant possessions and abilities to help a person disobey Him. Using His gifts in that way is a transgression and a betrayal, for which the person is answerable.

The heedless person follows appetite, temperament, and the self’s demands without seeking Allah’s pleasure or nearness. The loss is the wasting of life’s priceless hours and the best opportunity for gain. In mercy, Allah calls believers who affirm His oneness to the five prayers, bringing together many forms of worship so that every word, action, movement, and stillness can receive its share of His gifts.

Passage 8 of 59 · English edition pp. 25–26

The heart’s presence and the king’s door

Prayer’s secret and centre are the heart’s complete turning toward Allah. A distracted worshipper resembles a visitor arriving at a king’s door to apologise, ask for generosity and mercy, and seek nourishment that will strengthen him for service. Just as the audience is about to begin, he turns aside—or turns his back—and busies himself with what the king most dislikes and values least. He gives that thing his heart’s attention and sends his servants to stand in his place, apologise, and serve on his behalf. Those servants represent the limbs.

The king sees this, yet his generosity prevents him from dismissing the attendants entirely. Some benefit still reaches them. But there is a difference between an entitled share of the spoils and a discretionary gift to someone without such a share. Qur’an 46:19 is cited: people have degrees according to their deeds and will be repaid without injustice.

The author says humanity was created for Allah, while other things were created for humanity’s use. He then cites two reports addressed to the “son of Adam.” The first says, in meaning: I created you for Myself and everything else for you; do not let what I created for you distract you from the purpose for which I created you. The second urges the servant not to treat that purpose as play or exhaust himself in anxiety over provision, to seek Allah, and to recognise that finding Him is finding everything, while missing Him is losing everything; it ends by declaring Him more beloved than all else.

These reports are retained here because they occur in the text. The English editor says they could not be traced in hadith collections; their inclusion is not authentication as divine speech. The passage closes by presenting prayer as a means to nearness, intimate address, love, and companionship with Allah.

What happens between prayers

Between prayers, heedlessness, estrangement, hardness, turning away, slips, and sins can distance a servant from the Lord. The author compares this condition to becoming a stranger to servanthood, or surrendering to an enemy who chains the person in the prison of self and desire.

The person experiences constriction, worries, grief, sorrow, and regret without necessarily recognising their cause. The loving Lord’s mercy therefore establishes a comprehensive act of worship. Passage 10 of 59 · English edition pp. 27–28

Purification before prayer

Wuḍūʾ cleanses the body and the limbs that will worship. Its inward meaning is repentance, cleansing the heart of the dirt of sins. Qur’an 2:222 joins Allah’s love for those who repent with His love for those who purify themselves.

The text gives the testimony of faith after ablution and the supplication asking Allah to make the person one of those who repeatedly repent and purify themselves. Water removes outward dirt; repentance removes sins; the testimony removes association of partners with Allah. Together, they complete inward and outward degrees of purification and servanthood before standing before Him.

Having been purified, the servant is permitted to stand in the divine presence and return from flight. Coming to Allah’s house places the servant among those who serve Him. This explains why attendance at the mosque completes prayer’s worship—obligatory according to some scholars, recommended according to others.

Passage 12 of 59 · English edition pp. 29–30

The opening declaration of greatness

The worshipper faces the Sacred House with the face and Allah with the heart, leaving the earlier turning away behind. The author describes standing in need and surrender, lowering the head and gaze, yielding the hands, and keeping the whole heart directed to Him without turning aside even for a moment. The worshipper then declares Allah’s greatness with reverence. Heart and tongue should agree: Allah must be greater in the heart than everything else. The claim is made true by setting aside what distracts from Him. If another concern occupies the heart instead, the author says that concern is being treated as more important, while the declaration remains only on the tongue.

When the heart follows the declaration, two barriers are addressed. The servant lays aside the cloak of pride, which contradicts servanthood, and ceases turning inwardly toward other attachments. Genuine reverence for Allah prevents both self-exaltation and distraction from Him.

Seeking protection before recitation

Recitation begins with seeking Allah’s protection from Satan. The author describes prayer as one of the servant’s most honourable and beneficial stations, in this life and the next. Satan first tries to prevent bodily participation. If he cannot, he tries to interrupt the heart with intrusive thoughts so that the servant fails to give worship its due.

The passage expresses this as an assurance, in meaning: seek My shelter, and I will guard you and suffice you against this enemy.

Imagine a king who draws someone close and addresses him, only for that person to turn his back or look around without understanding the king’s words. The author asks how much more serious such disregard is before the true King, Lord of the worlds and Sustainer of heaven and earth.

Passage 16 of 59 · English edition pp. 33–35

Al-Fātiḥah as an address

The worshipper is asked to pause briefly at each verse and attend to the answer described in the hadith, as though hearing it. This is contemplative attention to a transmitted response, not an instruction to expect an audible voice.

After “All praise belongs to Allah, Lord of the worlds,” the response is that the servant has praised Him. After “The Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful,” the servant has extolled Him. After “Master of the Day of Judgement,” the servant has glorified His majesty. “You alone we worship, and You alone we ask for help” is shared between Lord and servant. The request for guidance through the end of the chapter is for the servant, who is granted what is asked. The hadith is linked in the guide to Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 395a.

Someone who has tasted prayer understands why other words cannot simply replace the opening takbīr or al-Fātiḥah, any more than other actions can replace standing, bowing, or prostration. Each act, and each verse of al-Fātiḥah, has its particular worship, effect, and inward experience.

“All praise belongs to Allah” affirms every perfection in His names, attributes, and actions and declares Him free of faults in each. His actions are wisdom, mercy, benefit, and justice; His attributes are perfect and majestic; His names are beautiful.

Passage 17 of 59 · English edition pp. 35–37

What praise includes

The author describes divine praise as filling this world and the next, the heavens and earth and everything within and between them. Creation and command come from it and stand through it. Existence and non-existence, the sending of messengers, the revealing of books, the creation and inhabiting of Paradise and Hell—all bear witness to His praise. Even obedience, disobedience, a falling leaf, and an atom’s movement fall within the creation over which He is praised. This is a claim about His wisdom and sovereignty, not approval of disobedience.

Allah remains worthy of praise in Himself even if servants do not praise Him, just as He remains the One and the true God even if people do not acknowledge or worship Him. The author cites the report that Allah speaks through His Prophet’s tongue in the words announcing that He hears whoever praises Him. Allah is the One who enables the servant’s heart and tongue to praise; thus praise, dominion, goodness, and the return of every hidden and manifest affair belong to Him.

These observations are described as a drop from an immense sea. The ability to praise is itself a blessing deserving further praise, and that further praise calls for more again. A lifetime of breaths would not discharge the thanks owed for a single blessing. No collection of praises exhausts what is due to Him.

Al-Awzāʿī is quoted as hearing a singer in a bathhouse express gratitude either for a blessing received or for an affliction averted. The servant should recognise the inability to praise adequately and the fact that even the praise offered was inspired by Allah; without Him, no one would be guided.

Praise extends to every inward and outward circumstance, welcome or unwelcome, and to the affairs of all creation—the righteous and the wicked, the higher and lower worlds—even when the servant cannot see the wisdom involved. People offer more or less praise according to their knowledge of their Lord. The passage closes with the intercession report: in prostration, the Prophet is inspired with praises that had not previously occurred to him.

Passage 18 of 59 · English edition pp. 38–39

Lordship and mercy

“Lord of the worlds” calls the worshipper to recognise Allah’s sole lordship. He creates, provides, arranges affairs, brings beings into existence, and enriches them. Therefore He alone is their God, the One they worship and the refuge to whom they turn in distress. There is no other Lord or true object of worship.

The Arabic transcription includes the discussion of “The Most Merciful, the Especially Merciful” within this heading. Mercy encompasses everything, with every creature receiving a share. The author then distinguishes the particular mercy that enables a servant to stand in prayer, address the Lord through His words, seek His favour, ask for guidance, and request the completion of blessings in this life and the next.

He cites an unspecified report about Gabriel being told each night to raise one person for worship and let another sleep. It illustrates the enabling mercy of worship; the passage does not supply a chain or establish its authenticity. A garbled phrase in the online Arabic occurs around this report and is left visible in the source view.

As praise and knowledge encompass all things, so does mercy. Qur’an 40:7 is cited. The author also notes that a person may miss the particular mercy of being enabled to worship, even while creation receives mercy generally.

Why worship comes before seeking help

Worship is the action and purpose directed to Allah; help is what the servant needs to fulfil it. Allah is both the One worshipped and the One whose assistance is sought. “You alone we worship” includes useful knowledge leading to Him, love, truthful intention, sincerity, and righteous action. Worship that is neither for Allah nor through His aid comes to nothing. The next sentence in the online transcription appears to have lost a negation: as printed, it condemns reliance upon Allah alone, contrary to the entire surrounding argument and the English rendering. Each clause brings benefit and guards against a failure opposed to servanthood. Together they place the servant within its reality: an exclusive purpose and dependence upon the One who makes it possible.

Passage 25 of 59 · English edition pp. 45–47

Ten needs for guidance, and three responses to it

First, past beliefs, intentions, or actions may have been mistaken: repentance from them is guidance. Second, a person may know the foundation of something but need its details. Third, someone may be guided in one aspect but need completion in another, adding guidance to guidance. Fourth, the future calls for the same guidance already received in the past.

Fifth, a person may have no settled belief on a matter and need a sound one. Sixth, a false belief may need to be removed and replaced by its opposite. Seventh, someone may be capable of a good act but lack the will to do it. Eighth, someone may be willing but unable and need capability. Ninth, both capability and willingness may be absent. Tenth, a person already sound in belief, intention, knowledge, and action needs continued steadiness.

This is why asking for guidance is described as the greatest and most urgent need. The merciful Lord requires the request repeatedly, day and night, in the five prayers, when the servant stands in a particularly good state. The English edition omits the separate eighth case; the Arabic includes it.

The path of those given guidance differs from the paths of those under anger and those astray. The author explicitly names Jews and Christians, along with others, within this inherited exegetical discussion. That naming is part of the source and is retained here as his interpretation, rather than silently omitted or used to label a reader’s neighbours.

He distinguishes three responses. Those favoured receive guidance and remain in it, with different shares according to its details. Those astray lack the knowledge and practice of guidance and are lost in seeking the path. Those under anger know the truth but turn from acting upon it. The author then returns to the comparison between prayer and listening, noting that a fuller treatment would take him beyond the subject.

Passage 27 of 59 · English edition pp. 48

Bowing

Bowing is submission to the Lord’s greatness, awe before His majesty, and humility before His might. The servant bends the back, lowers the head and stature, and praises Him with words of glorification and reverence.

Heart, limbs, and speech gather in submission. This union of humility, remembrance, and exaltation distinguishes worship of the Lord from people’s deference to one another. Submission belongs to the servant; greatness belongs to the Lord. Bowing is fulfilled when humility before Allah removes the heart’s exaltation of itself or other creatures and gives that place to Him alone.

Passage 30 of 59 · English edition pp. 50–51

Prostration of body and heart

With the takbīr, the servant descends into prostration and gives each part of the body its share of worship. The forehead, face, and nose are placed upon the earth; the most honoured part of the body is humbled before the Lord. The heart likewise turns, yields, returns, and acknowledges need before His greatness and might. The body’s upper parts come down toward its lower parts.

The inward and outward states agree: the heart prostrates as do the face, nose, hands, knees, and feet. The thighs are kept from resting on the shins, the belly from the thighs, and the upper arms from the sides, so that each part bears its own share rather than being carried by another. These are the author’s descriptions of the posture, rather than a survey of legal variations.

He cites the hadith that the servant is nearest the Lord while prostrating (Muslim 482). Because the heart’s prostration means complete submission, it can continue beyond the bodily act. The passage asks a predecessor whether the heart prostrates; the answer follows under the next heading in the online edition.

Five names for prayer and the pattern of a rakʿah

The predecessor answers that the heart can make a prostration from which it does not rise until meeting Allah. The source note attributes this saying, through Ibn Taymiyyah, to Sahl ibn ʿAbd Allāh al-Tustarī. It means continuing humility, repentance, presence, and awareness of Allah, alone and in company.

Prayer is built upon recitation, standing, bowing, prostration, and remembrance. The Qur’an names the whole prayer through each: standing in 73:2 and 2:238; recitation in 17:78 and 73:20; bowing in 2:43 and 77:48; prostration in 15:98 and 96:19; remembrance in 62:9 and 63:9. The online footnote misprints one recitation reference as 73:48; the cited words belong to 73:20.

Prostration is its noblest action, and Qur’anic recitation its noblest remembrance. The first revealed surah begins with the command to read and ends with prostration and nearness. The author sees a rakʿah arranged on that pattern: recitation at its beginning and prostration at its end.

Passage 33 of 59 · English edition pp. 55–56

Forgiveness, the debtor, and the second prostration

The Prophet repeatedly asks, “My Lord, forgive me,” in this sitting. The author asks the worshipper to imagine being guarantor for a debtor who delays and deceives. The debt is demanded from the debtor, but the guarantor is also answerable. He therefore seeks help in obtaining what is owed so that he can be released from the demand. The image explains asking for help against one’s own self.

Heart and self share good and evil, reward and punishment, praise and blame. The self tends to flee servanthood and neglect duties toward Allah and people. If its power prevails, the heart becomes its partner and captive; if the heart prevails, the self becomes the heart’s partner and captive.

Kneeling before Allah, the servant seeks mercy, forgiveness, guidance, provision, and wellbeing. Together these gather the good of this life and the next and protection from harm in both. Provision sustains body, heart, and spirit and brings worldly and next-life benefit. Wellbeing wards off worldly harm. Guidance brings next-life benefit and reaches every detail of affairs. Forgiveness wards off harm in both lives; mercy gathers all these gifts.

A second prostration is prescribed because of its excellence, its deep expression of servanthood, and the nearness it brings. It seals the rakʿah, with what precedes preparing for it. The author compares its place to the pilgrimage’s ṭawāf of visitation. He recalls Ibn ʿUmar declining to answer a marriage proposal for his daughter while circling the Kaʿbah, then explaining afterward that they had been presenting themselves before Allah. Bowing comes first as a gradual approach to the higher act of prostration.

Passage 34 of 59 · English edition pp. 57–58

Why the acts are repeated

Prayer’s words and actions nourish heart and spirit as successive mouthfuls and drinks nourish the body. A single bite does not satisfy a hungry person; it may awaken hunger further. The author quotes a predecessor comparing hurried prayer without stillness to taking only a mouthful or two from a meal.

Repeating an act renews servanthood and nearness. The second performance can be gratitude for the first. Repetition increases goodness, faith, knowledge, attention, strength of heart, and openness of the chest, while removing dirt like a garment washed repeatedly. The author regards this as a small glimpse of a far greater divine wisdom, mercy, and gentleness in creation and command.

When the actions are complete and departure approaches, a final sitting is prescribed for praise of the Lord. The finest greeting in it is al-taḥiyyāt, the honours that belong to Allah alone.

Passage 35 of 59 · English edition pp. 58–59

The meaning of al-taḥiyyāt

Kings are greeted through words and acts of submission: some receive prostration, others praise, others wishes for long life and lasting rule, and some receive all three. The true King, before whom everything else perishes, is uniquely worthy of all such honours in their complete sense.

The word al-taḥiyyāt has been explained through kingship and through enduring life. The author joins these meanings: Allah is King and possesses dominion, so every royal honour belongs to Him in truth. The plural with the definite article gathers the entire class of honours.

He also discusses the word’s formation from ḥayāh, “life”: an earlier form with adjoining y sounds contracts into taḥiyyah. Greetings such as “May your life endure,” “Live ten thousand years,” “May your days continue,” and “May your life be prolonged” all express a desire for continuing life and rule. Absolute, unending life and dominion belong only to the Living, Self-Sustaining One. Every other king dies and every other kingdom passes away.

What belongs to the Good

Goodness concerns description, action, speech, and relationship. The author gives examples of things honoured by their attribution to Allah: His house, His servant, His spirit, His she-camel, and His Garden, the home of the good. These examples retain their distinct meanings; attribution does not make created things divine.

Every good thing belongs to Him, is with Him, comes from Him, and returns to Him. He is good and accepts only what is good. He is the Lord of the good, and those dwelling in His abode of honour are good.

Passage 38 of 59 · English edition pp. 61–62

The finest words after the Qur’an

The author lists five expressions: glorifying Allah, praising Him, affirming that no other is God, declaring His greatness, and acknowledging that there is no power or strength except through Him. The passage then explains the first four in detail; the fifth is named but not separately expounded here.

Glorification declares Him free of every fault, deficiency, evil, and likeness to created beings. Praise affirms every perfection of word, action, and attribute, fully and eternally. The testimony of oneness affirms His exclusive divinity and the invalidity of other objects of worship. Seeking ultimate shelter in another is compared to taking a spider’s house as protection from heat and cold: it cannot provide the shelter sought.

Declaring His greatness acknowledges Him as greater, more majestic, mightier, stronger, more protective, more capable, more knowing, and wiser than everything else. These expressions in their complete meaning belong to Him alone.

Passage 40 of 59 · English edition pp. 63–64

The two testimonies

The testimony of Allah’s oneness is the foundation upon which prayer rests, and prayer is among its rights. It is joined to testimony that the Messenger was sent by Allah. The author cites Ibn Masʿūd’s statement that after saying this, the prayer is completed and one may rise or remain seated.

He records two legal understandings: some Kufan scholars take completion literally at that point, while scholars of the Hijaz and others take it to mean approaching completion. Under either reading, testimony stands near the prayer’s conclusion. It is also prescribed at the close of life; he cites the report promising Paradise to the person whose last words affirm that there is no god but Allah.

Likewise, the two testimonies conclude ablution. Near the completion of prayer, the servant is permitted to ask for personal needs.

Passage 46 of 59 · English edition pp. 67–69

What the heart attends to at each movement

While standing, the heart attends to Allah’s sustaining power and greatness and does not turn aside. At the takbīr, it attends to His majesty. The opening supplication declares Him free of what does not befit Him and praises His perfection and the splendour of His countenance. Seeking refuge attends to His firm protection, sovereignty, defence of His servant, and guarding against the enemy.

During recitation, the worshipper knows Allah through His words. A predecessor is quoted as saying that Allah makes Himself known to His servants in His speech. The author describes different degrees of perception through metaphors of clear sight, partial sight, blindness, deafness, and impaired sight. These are his images for reception of meaning. Attention is directed to Allah, His attributes and actions, commands and prohibitions, judgements and names.

Bowing attends to His greatness, majesty, and might, hence the formula glorifying the Magnificent Lord. Rising attends to praise, glorification, servanthood, and His sole giving and withholding. Prostration attends to nearness, humility, need, brokenness, and earnest appeal. Sitting between prostrations attends to His sufficiency and generosity and the servant’s urgent need for forgiveness, mercy, wellbeing, guidance, and provision.

The final sitting resembles the pilgrim’s farewell circuit. The heart anticipates leaving prayer’s shelter for worldly ties and distractions whose burdens it knew before entering. Having tasted nearness and relief, it grieves at leaving and wishes the prayer could extend to the day of meeting Allah.

The author contrasts this address, in which happiness is found, with human dealings that can bring hurt, worry, grief, constriction, darkness, missed good deeds, acquired sins, and scattered attention. He says this difference is recognised by a heart alive with remembrance, love, and companionship with Allah and aware of those burdens.

Passage 47 of 59 · English edition pp. 70

Surrender and the meaning of Islam

The servant lives under two kinds of divine judgement. The first is what Allah decrees in inward and outward circumstances; each circumstance calls for a fitting act of servanthood. The second is the religious command governing what the servant should do.

Both require surrender of oneself to Allah. The author connects the name Islam with this surrender. A Muslim meets both decree and command through worship and obedience. Surrender to decree does not mean giving free rein to desire, appetite, or sin and then saying, “It was decreed for me.” The distinction is essential to the argument.

Passage 48 of 59 · English edition pp. 70–71

What prayer restores

Remembrance, Allah’s words, love, and worship settle the heart with its Lord, bring it near, and give it comfort. Faith brings security and excellence brings happiness. The author treats these as necessities of true life, success, and happiness.

Four pressures weaken that share: the self that commands evil, desire demanding satisfaction, natural dispositions pressing their claims, and Satan’s misguidance. The merciful Lord prescribes prayer to replace what has been lost and renew worn resolve and faith. The interval between prayers allows recovery, while prayer washes away what has accumulated.

Prayer’s form expresses humility, submission, obedience, and surrender, with each limb receiving its share. Its fruit and spirit are turning to Allah with the whole person. Its reward is entry into His presence, and preparing oneself for that audience recalls the greater presentation before Him on the Day of Resurrection.

Passage 49 of 59 · English edition pp. 71–72

The fruit of prayer

Fasting purifies the self; zakāh purifies wealth; pilgrimage brings forgiveness; striving in Allah’s cause surrenders to Him the self He has purchased for Paradise. Prayer’s fruit is the servant’s turning toward Allah and Allah’s turning toward the servant. The author sees the fruits of these other acts gathered within that encounter.

He notes that the Prophet describes the comfort of his eyes as placed “in” prayer, rather than in fasting, pilgrimage, or ʿumrah. Entering matters: a lover finds comfort in reunion, and a frightened person in entering safety. The comfort of actual entry is fuller than anticipation from outside. When speaking of relief from fatigue and burdens, the Prophet asks Bilāl to give rest through prayer.

Passage 50 of 59 · English edition pp. 72–75

Rest through prayer and nearness beyond reward

Prayer offers rest from worldly preoccupations like a tired traveller reaching home and safety. The author stresses rest “through” prayer, not relief “from” it. A reluctant worshipper whose heart is occupied elsewhere may experience prayer as an interruption, rush it, abandon stillness and humility, and say with the tongue what the heart does not share. He waits to leave and return to what actually pleases him.

For this person, prayer resembles a heavy restraint upon the limbs and a narrow prison for the heart. Yet it can still restrain destructive behaviour and bring expiation, reward, and mercy according to the worship offered; shortcomings may also incur punishment.

For another, prayer is a garden, delight, bodily rest, and joy of the spirit. This person shares the reward of others, receives its higher degrees, and is additionally granted particular nearness and standing. The author distinguishes reward from being drawn close by recalling Pharaoh’s promise to the magicians of both payment and proximity (26:41; 7:114).

He illustrates the difference through two visitors to a king’s house. One enters but remains behind a screen of desires, self-preoccupation, and wishful thinking. The sickened heart cannot enjoy the audience and longs to leave for its immediate pleasures. The other has the screen lifted, serves gladly, receives gifts from every direction, and is drawn close. The heart settles, the limbs become humble, and the visitor delights in conversation and wishes not to depart.

The author ends by seeking Allah’s guidance and help and calling this only a brief indication of prayer’s inward taste and one of its secrets.

Passage 52 of 59 · English edition pp. 76–77

The challenge to musical listening

The author asks, by Allah, whether the listeners find the experience just described in their gatherings—or whether those gatherings even leave them able to find a small part of it in prayer. He intensifies the challenge by asking whether they have ever sensed so much as its scent. He states his own conviction that their experience is opposed to it, while declining a longer description to avoid extending the work.

He contrasts Qur’anic verses with poetic verses, standing before the Lord of the worlds with standing before singers, and delight in remembrance and Allah’s words with delight in songs and their meanings. His language is deliberately severe: he calls the latter an incitement to sexual wrongdoing and “Satan’s Qur’an.” These are the author’s polemical descriptions, not neutral labels applied by this guide to every form of sound.

He swears that the two cannot occupy one heart without one expelling the other, then invokes the report that the Messenger’s daughter and the daughter of Allah’s enemy would not be joined in marriage to one man. The analogy is retained because it forms part of his argument. He closes by acknowledging that Allah knows best.

Passage 53 of 59 · English edition pp. 78–80

Practices that cultivate the heart

The author asks how sound spiritual experience can come to hearts that have departed from the Prophet’s guidance and the practice of his Companions and the early generations. He lists their sources of such experience: prescribed prayer; reading, reflecting upon, and hearing the Qur’an and seeking its reward; sitting closely with scholars; striving in Allah’s cause; enjoining good and forbidding wrong; and love and aversion ordered by faith, with the actions that follow.

He contrasts this with later gatherings centred on pipes, frame drums, flutes, songs from attractive performers, dancing, noise, and raised voices, while neglecting worship that runs against the self’s preferences. The Arabic develops its contrast through wordplay: melodies and Qur’an; lute and ṭunbūr against faith and light; pipes against al-Zumar; the reed flute against the opening of al-Qamar; flutes against Yā Sīn and al-Ṣāffāt; sung verse against al-Shuʿarāʾ; whistling and clapping against the prophets’ way.

It continues with songs about dark eyes, waists, and figures against Yūnus and Hūd; standing in Satan’s service against serving the Merciful through al-Anʿām and al-Aʿrāf; stringed instruments against the great Qur’an and the seven oft-repeated verses; and feet arrayed in the musical gathering against feet arrayed before the Merciful. Some wording in the online transcription is irregular, so these are explanations of the contrasted terms rather than claims to reproduce every pun in English.

He praises Allah for distinguishing these experiences and for sustaining both groups in this life while differentiating their honour on the Day of Resurrection. He repeats his oath that the two loves do not share a heart and repeats the marriage analogy. A closing couplet warns that love can make a person its captive and urges care in choosing whom to love.

Passage 54 of 59 · English edition pp. 81–83

The listening of the Companions

When the Companions gathered and wanted their hearts moved toward the One they loved, they would ask one person to recite while the others listened. Their hearts settled, their eyes overflowed, and they found sweetness of faith. ʿUmar would ask Abū Mūsā to remind them of their Lord, and his recitation worked upon their hearts. ʿUthmān is quoted as saying that purified hearts would never have enough of Allah’s words.

The author asks how lovers could be satisfied with the words of their Beloved, which contain what they seek, and says their hearts were opened by the Qur’an rather than melodies. He quotes verse describing remembrance as medicine whose abandonment worsens illness. Another couplet says even the desert lizard and the fish might meet, but revelation and the reed pipe do not. Both express his contrast, rather than a general statement about animals or music.

He describes a person attentive to Allah, longing to meet Him, ready to understand His intent and apply the words to the person’s own state, listening to a skilled reciter with a beautiful voice. His example is Ṭā Hā 20:1–7: the Qur’an was not sent to cause distress but to remind those who stand in awe; it comes from the Creator of earth and the high heavens; the Merciful is established over the Throne; everything above, below, and between belongs to Him; and He knows speech, secrets, and what is more hidden.

When such words meet a truthful, living heart that knows love, they are like reunion after separation, cold water in intense thirst, or abundant rain upon land at its moment of greatest need. The land produces beautiful plants, standing in gratitude and praise.

He asks whether these two forms of listening and their inward tastes can be equal before Allah, His angels, His Messenger, and truthful servants. He describes the criticised listeners as pursuing the self’s appetite. Whoever cannot distinguish the experiences is urged to ask sincerely for a living heart, light against ignorance, and discernment between truth and falsehood. Allah is near and answers.

Passage 56 of 59 · English edition pp. 84–86

Why discomfort may be noticed later

The author says a sincere person moved by poetic listening may afterward feel constriction, estrangement, distance, interruption, and darkness. A person with little inward life may not notice this—he quotes the image that a wound does not hurt the dead. Absorption in the experience can also prevent recognition of its cause. Fair examination, he argues, reveals the source of the discomfort.

Even in its best case, he describes this listening as mixing truth and falsehood, desire and doubt. The spirit’s good share is mixed with the self’s, desire’s, and Satan’s. In his account, appetite is foundational to the gathering and any benefit is incidental. A little clean water enters the heart’s valley alongside much muddy water: the bad overwhelms the good, or the two remain together.

A sincere person’s attention to what is sought can hide the mixture during the gathering. After the intoxication of the experience passes, its residue becomes perceptible. The more alive and sincere the heart, the more clearly it may notice the effect, even without recognising where it came from.

The author compares this to someone fully absorbed in a beloved sight, a frightening sight, or a pleasure. A blow or sting may scarcely register at the time. Once attention is released, the pain is felt as though the injury had just occurred. The absence of felt pain during absorption did not mean there was no injury.

Passage 57 of 59 · English edition pp. 86–87

Honey and the vessel that carries it

Some sincere people, the author says, hasten after such gatherings to repentance, forgiveness, and remedies for estrangement. He considers recognition of this pattern a matter for discerning people concerned with the self’s illnesses and their treatment, and asks Allah for help.

He explicitly allows that a sincere listener to poetry may experience a sound taste of faith. His objection is that this is like drinking honey from an impure vessel. A truthful person of high aspiration seeks a vessel suited to the drink, and if none is available, preserves the drink rather than pouring it into an unsuitable one.

Other people use whatever vessel is available. The author’s deliberately disturbing examples include bones of a dead animal, dog or pig skin, and a vessel long used for wine; the online text also contains an awkward rhetorical reference to a crow drinking. These details express revulsion at the means, not a claim that the sweetness itself is imaginary.

During absorption, honey’s sweetness hides the vessel’s stench and its effect upon the heart. Afterward, a sincere person worshipping for Allah and through His help may feel constriction. By contrast, someone seeking only personal appetite is described as drinking impurity from a filthy vessel without noticing, under the dominance of self, desire, and Satan.

The listener who tastes the Qur’an receives a pure, good drink in a clean and fitting vessel. The author distinguishes three vessels—clean, impure, and mixed—and three drinks—pure, impure, and blended. These are separate dimensions of the comparison.

Passage 58 of 59 · English edition pp. 88

Three hearts and the consequences of means

A sound heart receives a pure drink in a clean vessel. A diseased heart receives an impure drink in a dirty vessel. A heart containing both faith and hypocrisy receives according to those two substances. Allah has appointed a measure for everything.

The discerning person examines causes through their ends and consequences, looking at purposes and what things lead to. The author concludes that understanding the law’s blocking of means to forbidden acts establishes, in his view, the prohibition of this listening.

He then gives the example of an unrelated woman, hearing her voice, and being alone with her, using broad prohibitory wording. This source claim is preserved rather than omitted. It is the author’s argument in this passage; this guide does not present it as a complete account of the legal distinctions and scholarly discussion surrounding women’s voices, ordinary interaction, or listening.

Passage 59 of 59 · English edition pp. 88–89

Two grounds of prohibition and the closing prayer

The author distinguishes what is prohibited because of the harm it contains from what is prohibited because it leads to harmful wrongdoing. Looking only at the outward act while ignoring what it leads to can make a prohibition difficult to understand.

The treatise closes by acknowledging that Allah knows best, praising the Lord of the worlds, and asking for blessings and peace upon Muhammad, the chief of the messengers, his family, Companions, and those who follow them with excellence until the Day of Judgement. It ends with an appeal to Allah’s generosity and mercy.
